## Code Katas and Dojos

> I had a simple thing I wanted to try, and I tried it many times. I looked for feedback each time so I could work to
> improve. There was no pressure: the code was effectively throwaway. It was fun: I kept making small steps forward,
> which motivated me to continue. Finally, I came out of it knowing more than when I went in. [^codekatas]

[^codekatas]: Dave Thomas on
his [blog about how he started with Code Katas](http://codekata.com/kata/codekata-how-it-started/)

Code katas are little practices to teach developers how to play with a coding problem. The meeting in which one or more
developers practice a kata is a dojo. Historically (and in my experience) this often comes with Test
Driven Development style. While I very much appreciate this working style, this post is focused on the surrounding
format.

Understanding the problem, thinking about its boundaries, thinking about how to solve it, trying out a solution, making
a mistake, testing a hypothesis, validating correctness. All of those steps and skills can be practiced without the
daily pressure to perform or achieve. Without any pressure there is safety for curiosity to try out new paths, to solve
a problem in multiple ways and implement it using approaches that might not seem suitable for features that are expected
in time.

Looking at the martial arts origin of the word, there is more than exploration to katas, there is internalization.
When I started learning Python, within the coding dojos I could both explore the endless possibilities Python provides,
and I could also internalize the language to easily access the patterns and data structures.

Beyond learning how to handle a technology or tool, within a kata I practice soft skills. How to look up internals
or documentation? How to set up tests and environment? How do I break down the problem into small parts, and with which
part should one start? How can I build the feature having in mind that I need to add more soon? How do I design the
test?

I joined a dojo for this internalization of a new language, and stayed with them for the soft skills I could practice
there — but to explain what those are, I need to talk about mob programming.

## Software Teaming (aka Mob Programming)

> Software Teaming nudges the Agile idea of working together a bit further by introducing the notion that designing,
> writing code, testing, and other tasks can be done with a group of people at the same computer. [^software_teaming]

[^software_teaming]: Zuill, Woody; Meadows, Kevin. Software Teaming: A Mob Programming, Whole-Team Approach

All the teams I worked in shared the idea that they work on the same code base toward a common goal, but rarely did
the whole team pursue that shared goal together in a group session; but I did made the experience that coding in a group
even increases all the benefits that Katas (or just pair programming) already offer!

### Learning together

Having a team to discuss the solutions comes with a lot of knowledge sharing, not only about the domain and the
problem itself, but also about the historic changes in the codebase that prepared or hinder the new requirements. It
offers an opportunity to share coding standards or architecture guidelines that are often only implicitly defined (or to
question those). Pairing with any number of participants is an invitation to learn more about the codebase than one
could alone.

It also gives the opportunity to practice methodologies in a group, like Test Driven Development. Weather it is to try a
new style, gathering experience and opinions from all participants, or to train habits to improve the group's standards.

### The driver and the navigator

One can structure pairing on a problem in different ways; the most common pattern is the driver–navigator split.
One person is the navigator, who thinks about the problem, its boundaries, and how the solution should look;
another person is the driver, who implements the solution as guided by the intent defined by the navigator.
The roles are not fixed, and at the latest when the driver is bursting with ideas of their own, the roles should be
rotated.

The goal of this split is to not type an idea down directly, but to first think it through, giving the group the
opportunity to understand and comment, and only then implement the reviewed solution. The benefit of working with
multiple people is that there are several ideas on how to solve something, several perspectives on what can be
important, and several knowledge bases about the domain or the code.

### The soft skills of working in a team

The setup I just described requires a non-trivial skill set that are the foundation of respectful teamwork.

> **Kindness**: Act in a kindly way. Be warm-hearted, gentle, and polite. Display a pleasant disposition to others. Show
> concern for others.
>
> **Consideration**: Consider your team members’ ideas. To do this, we need to be able to listen. To
> listen requires humility. Our own idea is only one possible way. We need to try the other ideas rather than simply
> finding reasons not to try them. We also need to consider each others’ feelings. Considering others’ feelings is much
> more difficult than considering their ideas. We don’t have easy answers for making this happen. We encourage everyone
> to
> explore it themselves.
>
> **Respect**: Respect is like a diplomatic protocol. It’s about allowing others to keep their
> self-esteem. We can disagree and still be respectful. Disagreements generate ideas, but those ideas can’t flourish in
> a
> disrespectful environment. [^software_teaming]

Those are all skills I wish everyone had, but I can't say I have mastered them. It is one thing to write
about safety and being open to ideas; the actual skills are:

* to be open for questions. Every "stupid" question is most probably an open question for three other people in the
  room, with only one having the courage to ask
* to listen to an idea, identify its intention, and consider every aspect of it (because maybe just a part of
  it solves a problem that is not solved yet)
* to be empathetic enough to acknowledge the effort the person made in sharing their idea, and to let them speak at
  their own speed and in their own voice
* to be humble enough to consider their idea over my own, giving credit to the person who raised it
* to explain which parts of the idea fit and why they fit (or why not). Especially if I found a mistake of any kind,
  the ability to translate my critique into something that the other person will perceive as helpful, so they take
  away an understanding from my explanation
* to acknowledge everybody loses the ability to type without errors the moment someone is watching

## Dojos in the time of AI Agents?

Given today's AI agents, aren't solo coding sessions already pairings between a human navigator and an AI driver?
If the AI is driving anyway, do we even need the internalization of skills using katas?

Typing code sparks joy for me. On more and more workdays I find myself typing less code and more prompts. This
change is real and will probably change how I learn the next language I pick up. But the skills that are
internalized are not limited to the hard skills of writing in a language or using a tool correctly. The skills of
deconstructing a problem, comparing solutions, validating a proposed solution, and reviewing code not written by
oneself are exactly where AI shifts the bottleneck to, and those are the skills that are trained in dojos.

Additionally, there is now a new set of skills we developers must learn. How do we prompt agents? How do we identify
hallucinations or low quality solutions? How do we phrase a problem or task so that the agent implements a
sane amount of code to review? How do we improve the overall quality of a codebase with an agent that is trained to
match the quality it already finds there?

And one of the biggest challenges I experience with AI is that people talk less with each other, building up silos of
knowledge and opinions. Mob Programming can encourage exchange between developers and
build the soft skills to work in a team whose main task is not writing the code but agreeing on the best
solution for the problem.

And looking at dojos alone: the more strictly the driver was kept from proposing ideas, the more appealing it sounds
to hand the job of translating the navigator's intent into code over to an AI.

## Conclusion

I am thankful for the dojo experience I have at my current company. The technical and soft skills I was able to learn
(and still learn) are making me a better developer and a better teammate. The safety of the dojo gave me room to try
out new patterns and ways of working, to ask all the questions about Python, to question design decisions, and
(harder than I thought) to just shut up and watch somebody make a mistake they will spot themselves soon enough without
me pointing it out.

Heartfelt thanks to our Dojo organizer, who raised the question of benefits of dojos and by this inspired this post.

Happy Coding :)
